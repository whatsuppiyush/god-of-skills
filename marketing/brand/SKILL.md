# Brand

The discipline of shaping how a market perceives you so perceived value, willingness to pay,
conversion, and loyalty all rise. This skill turns "build a brand" into concrete, buildable
artifacts: a mission and story, a voice chart anyone can apply, a sharp position, a scored
name, and a perception audit, plus the guardrails for rebrands and AI use.

## When to use this
- Naming a company, product, or feature, or renaming after a domain conflict.
- Writing or fixing brand guidelines, a voice/tone doc, or onboarding writers and agencies.
- Positioning a "me too" product, or entering a market with a dominant incumbent.
- The brand feels generic, invisible in its category, or written by ten different people.
- Auditing perception: seen as capable but cold, or likeable but unreliable.
- Planning a launch stunt, reactive PR moment, or viral trend play on a tight budget.
- Debating a rebrand or logo refresh and worrying about losing recognition.
- Setting an AI-usage policy so the brand does not turn into sameness.

## When NOT to use this (reach for instead)
- For the actual words on a page, ad, or email (headlines, hooks, body copy): use
  **copywriting-messaging**. Brand sets the voice; copywriting writes inside it.
- To decide whether brand is even your priority right now, your channel mix, or stage-fit of
  the whole GTM plan: use **strategy-fundamentals**. Do not over-invest in brand before
  product-market fit (weight the earliest stage ~80% product validation / 20% brand).
- To measure whether a brand change moved anything: use **analytics-data**.

## How this works (decision path)
Sequence brand work against company stage, then pick the play by symptom.

1. **Stage gate first.** Product-dev stage: build the high-level framework and research the
   market. Validating stage: keep brand light, use the concept to guide tests. Early traction:
   formalize guidelines, refine, invest in content. Do not build a $150k identity pre-PMF.
2. **Foundations before expression.** If there is no mission, story, or voice yet, start with
   *Strategy & foundations* and *Voice & persona*. Everything else assumes these exist.
3. **Then differentiate.** If the brand exists but feels undifferentiated or invisible, run the
   *Positioning & differentiation* plays: find an enemy, invert one category norm, audit
   warmth vs competence.
4. **Naming is its own loop.** If the task is a name, go straight to *Naming*, it is a
   self-contained quantity-and-scoring process.
5. **Amplify, evolve, govern.** Use *Earned media* for attention, *Rebrand* when changing
   identity (protect recognition), and *AI governance* to decide where AI is allowed.

Diagnostic shortcuts:
- "We look like every competitor" -> zag (invert one norm) + challenger (pick an enemy).
- "Capable but nobody trusts / likes us" -> warmth vs competence audit.
- "Content sounds inconsistent" -> voice chart + brand muse.
- "Domain is taken / need a name" -> naming (generate 300+, score, respell, verify).

## Key numbers & benchmarks
- **Minimum-viable design:** spend no more than **$5,000** on first-pass logo/colors/fonts;
  reserve a full agency ($50k-$150k) for after product-market fit.
- **Brand story:** keep it under **250 words** (strong examples run ~140 and ~235 words).
- **Naming:** generate **300-500** candidates; a great name rarely appears in the first 20.
  Score on **8 metrics** (emotion, memorability, Googlability, spelling-when-heard, sayability,
  clarity, distinctiveness, .com availability).
- **Warmth + competence** explain roughly **half** of purchase intent, loyalty, and
  recommendation. "Grounded" products see ~**60% higher** willingness-to-pay among remote and
  urban buyers. For luxury, invert: prioritize competence, a little rejection raises desire.
- **Earned-media gate:** compute required impressions to beat paid CPMs before building.
  Example: ~$800k of vending machines needed **80-100M impressions** to beat $8-10 CPMs.
  Same-day reactive replies vastly outperform polished week-late ones (38M vs 4M views).
- **Rebrand cautionary numbers:** Tropicana lost **$30M+ in 60 days** and reverted; a legacy
  auto brand fell **-97.5% YoY** after a rebrand into a product vacuum. Expect **4-12 weeks**
  of backlash before judging results. Simplify logos that lose detail under **200px**.
- **AI-content sentiment:** favorability fell **60% -> 26%** (2023-2026); ~**50%** of consumers
  prefer brands that avoid gen-AI in customer-facing content; removing AI made content **15%**
  less similar and lifted engagement **3.5%**.
- **Distinctiveness outcomes (public):** one ice-cream redesign +50% sales; a neon-bottle olive
  oil reached a $240M valuation; a death-metal water brand hit $1.4B; a minimalist B&W tool hit
  $10B. Invert **one** element, not everything.

### Where to use AI in branding (and where not), the kitchen / dining room / concept framework

## The strategy
Generative AI makes brands sound the same, which is fatal for something whose job is to be distinctive. A three-layer framework decides where AI helps (behind the scenes) and where it quietly erodes brand equity (identity and voice).

## When to use it
Setting an AI-usage policy for a brand/content team; deciding whether to use AI for customer-facing copy or visuals; auditing where sameness is creeping into the brand.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Apply the three-layer framework** (issue #330):
   - **Kitchen (ops/prep):** use AI freely, research, drafts, prep, internal work. Nobody sees it.
   - **Dining room (presentation):** be cautious, AI-touched customer-facing output needs a human pass for taste and specificity.
   - **Concept (identity/positioning/voice):** never hand to AI, this is where AI yields sameness and where your differentiation lives.
2. **When you do use AI for assets, apply a brand-impact filter** (issue #277): product shots → lifestyle scenes (use negative prompts to protect labels), consistent team headshots from one photo, owned abstract key visuals. For every output ask: "is this *elevating* or *cheapening* the brand?"

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Supporting data (issue #330): AI-content favorability fell 60% → 26% (2023 → 2026); ~50% of consumers prefer brands that avoid gen-AI in customer-facing content. The Italy ChatGPT-ban natural experiment showed content became 15% less similar and engagement rose 3.5% when AI was removed.
- The "concept" layer is the line that matters most, outsourcing identity/voice to AI is how brands become interchangeable.
- Especially relevant to godofskills' own audience: AI *tools* users still need a human-owned brand voice.

## → Skill conversion note
Weak skill candidate, it's a governance framework, not an automatable tool. Best as a policy guide: classify each brand task into kitchen/dining/concept and route AI use accordingly.

### Build a brand on four elements, mission, story, voice, and a $5k minimum-viable design

## The strategy
A brand shapes perception to create emotional connection, which raises perceived value, willingness to pay, conversion, and LTV. The foundation breaks into four buildable elements: mission, story, voice, and design, with a deliberately cheap first pass on design.

## When to use it
Early-stage founders defining a brand, or teams codifying one that grew organically. Do the rough version during product development; refine after early traction.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Mission statement**, one sentence, about your contribution to the world, not what you sell.
   - Slack: "To make work life simpler, more pleasant, and more productive." Tesla: "To accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy."
2. **Brand story**, expand the mission into an emotional narrative under 250 words. Choose an origin framing (best when founders have an unusual background) or a consumer-focused framing (customer at the center). Examples: Who Gives A Crap ~235 words; BIC ~140 words.
3. **Brand voice**, how the company sounds as a person, applied across site, email, support, social, and ads. Build a voice chart: personality descriptors, tone, vocabulary preferences, and what NOT to sound like. References: Mailchimp ("experienced, compassionate business partner"), Tripadvisor ("we don't boss you around, or confuse you with jargon"), Dell ("frankness one would expect from a friend").
4. **Brand design (minimum viable brand)**, logo, a 2-4 color palette, 2-3 complementary fonts, iconography, custom imagery, and a cohesive site.
   - Spend no more than **$5,000** initially. Use 99designs, Upwork, or Fiverr for logo/colors/typography/iconography. Delay a custom website and custom photography (use builders + stock). Invest in a full agency ($50k, $150k) only after product-market fit.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Why it works: brands create a mental shortcut, allow price premiums (Starbucks beats McDonald's in market despite losing a blind coffee taste test), enable product expansion (people buy merch), and drive word-of-mouth.
- Liquid Death shows brand-as-product: edgy anti-corporate voice ("Murder your thirst"), skull design, merch/NFTs → $100M+ raised in 2 years.
- Hinge ("the dating app designed to be deleted") gets 78% as many engagements as Tinder on ~10% of the revenue, quality-of-brand effect.

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes a `brand-foundation` prompt: input company + audience, output a one-sentence mission, a <250-word story (origin or consumer framing), a voice chart, and a $5k MVB design brief.

### Run a 5-step brand strategy, research market, study competitors, write guidelines, invest in content, track response

## The strategy
A five-step operating loop for building and maintaining a brand, sequenced against company stage: heavy framework work during product development, light-touch during validation, then detailed refinement once early traction hits.

## When to use it
Any founder/marketer taking a brand from concept to consistency. Weight it 80% product validation / 20% brand framework in the earliest stage, don't over-invest in brand before PMF.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Research the target market** during product development. Survey/interview real segments (not assumed personas), do field research (observe behavior in relevant places), and review existing customer data for trends (location, age, demographics). Goal: learn values, preferences, and channels.
2. **Study competitors** alongside market research. Review their sites/newsletters, track tweets/mentions with TweetDeck, check the Facebook Ads Library, interview their users, and read G2/Trustpilot/YouTube reviews. Map their positioning, value props, voice, and engagement patterns to find a differentiation gap.
3. **Develop brand guidelines**, an internal doc covering mission, voice specs, and design standards (colors, fonts, logo usage). Rough version during validation, refined once you gain traction. Purpose: consistency across every writer/creator.
4. **Invest in content marketing**, the primary lever for conveying voice and story. Once you have early traction, hire one dedicated full-time content marketer (more invested and lower editing overhead than freelancers); grow into a team or agency later.
5. **Track consumer response** on public channels. Free tools: TweetDeck, Twitter advanced search, Google Alerts, and free tiers of Hootsuite/Mention. Watch both tagged interactions and untagged mentions; feed insights back into brand and product direction.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Timeline: Stage 1 (product dev) = build the high-level framework + research. Stage 2 (validating) = don't do heavy brand work; use the concept to guide tests and watch for unexpected customer segments. Stage 3 (early traction) = refine messaging/design, formalize guidelines, invest in content.
- Zendesk's 2016 rebrand shows evolution: from a Buddha mascot + bright green to geometric mini-logos that enabled storytelling across an expanded product line, while keeping a playful voice ("charming and humblident," no talking down, no hyperbole).
- Scale reference: Coca-Cola spends ~$4B/year on advertising just to maintain its brand.

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes a `brand-strategy-runbook` prompt: input product + stage, output the stage-appropriate action list (research questions, competitor teardown template, guidelines outline, content-hire trigger, monitoring stack).

### Name a brand by volume and metrics, generate 300-500, score on 8 criteria, then verify

## The strategy
Naming is a quantity game with a scoring rubric, not a flash of inspiration. Generate a huge pile using structured strategies, score each on defined metrics, salvage near-misses by respelling, then legally verify the survivors.

## When to use it
Naming a company, product, or feature; a rebrand; picking between shortlisted names; anytime the "obvious" name's domain is taken.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Generate 300-500 candidates** (issue #263, Art & Fear quantity principle). Sort by archetype, Descriptive / Abstract / Evocative / Functional-Fresh / Founder / Compound, and generate via Latin roots, translations, Litscape, and ChatGPT portmanteaus.
2. **Score each name on 8 metrics** (issue #220): emotion, memorability, Googlability, spelling-when-heard, sayability, clarity of purpose, distinctiveness, and .com availability. Generate via 8 strategies: mashups, wordplay, tribute, aspiration, nonsense words, descriptors, domain-as-name, and abbreviations (last resort).
3. **Narrow to ~30**, then to a shortlist. Feed strategies + product into ChatGPT and test candidates on your audience until an obvious winner emerges (the "labeling effect").
4. **Salvage-and-respell** to free up domains (issue #263): Quick→Quik, Light→Lyte, Drop→Dropp.
5. **Verify legally and technically:** run the final 6-point gut check, pronounceable, spellable, memorable, future-proof, extensible, pride, and clear via USPTO TESS + GoDaddy + domain history.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The quantity principle is the whole game: a great name almost never appears in your first 20 ideas. Push to hundreds.
- "Spelling-when-heard" and "sayability" are easy to skip and expensive to get wrong (word-of-mouth and voice search both punish unspellable names).
- Legal/domain verification is non-negotiable, a beloved name you can't own is a dead end.

## → Skill conversion note
Strong skill: input product description + archetype preferences, auto-generate 300+ names across the 8 strategies, score each on the 8 metrics, and return a ranked shortlist flagged for .com availability.

### Codify brand voice with a DO/DON'T chart, an archetype, and a "brand muse

## The strategy
"Be consistent" is useless without a test the whole team can apply. This card turns brand voice into three concrete artifacts, a DO/DON'T chart, a chosen archetype, and a real public figure to check every message against, so anyone can write on-brand.

## When to use it
Standing up a brand's voice; onboarding writers or agencies; when content across channels feels like it's written by different people; complements the `brand-foundations-four-elements` card's "voice" element.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Build a brand-voice chart** (issue #286): 3 DOs and 3 DON'Ts, and explicitly ban generic words (e.g. "authentic") so the chart actually constrains.
2. **Pick a persona framework** so the whole team recognizes the character (issue #103): a superhero archetype (Bruce Wayne problem-solver vs. Hulk righteous anger) or one of the 12 Jungian archetypes.
3. **Choose a "brand muse"** (issue #286): a real public figure whose voice yours resembles. Test every piece of messaging by asking "would [muse] say it this way?", a fast, shared consistency check.
4. **Write a short brand story** (issue #286): under 250 words, with emotional weight and conflict. Pick the origin-story angle if the founder background is interesting, or a consumer-focused angle if origins are straightforward (strong examples run 235 and 140 words).

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The brand muse is the sleeper tool: it collapses a subjective "is this on-brand?" debate into a concrete gut check anyone can run.
- Banning generic words in the DON'T column is what makes the chart bite, "authentic," "innovative," "seamless" belong on it.
- Keep the story tight: emotional weight comes from conflict and specificity, not length.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: interview the founder for tone preferences, then generate a filled DO/DON'T voice chart, a recommended archetype, 2-3 candidate brand muses, and a <250-word brand story draft.

### Sharpen positioning with an enemy, an Onlyness statement, and a contrarian "worse-here-better-there" trade-off

## The strategy
Challenger brands win by defining what they stand *against* and what they alone can claim. Pick an enemy, write an Onlyness statement, and deliberately be worse where rivals compete so you can be better somewhere that matters.

## When to use it
Entering a market with a dominant incumbent; a "me too" product that needs an edge; positioning resets where the brand feels undifferentiated.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Pick a brand "enemy"** (issue #116) to sharpen positioning: Hinge vs. Tinder ("designed to be deleted"); Chipotle "food with integrity" vs. fast-food; Liquid Death "death to plastic." The enemy can be a competitor, a category habit, or a status quo.
2. **Write an Onlyness statement** (issue #090): "the ONLY [category] that [differentiator]," then flesh it across What / How / Who / Where / Why / When (Trader Joe's example).
3. **Take a contrarian value position, deliberately worse where rivals fight, better elsewhere** (issue #147): Red Bull (small can, pricey, unusual taste → a new category); Nintendo Switch (underpowered but portable). Choose the axis you'll lose on so you can dominate the one you win on.
4. **Satirize a complacent incumbent** (issue #158): BRLO parodied Calvin Klein's inauthentic imagery to borrow fame and position as the authentic challenger. Works best against high-margin incumbents.
5. **Normalize-the-weird or weird-the-normal** (issue #148): make an unfamiliar product feel safe (juice shots as supplements) or add a distinctive twist to a mundane one (Liquid Death death-metal water, Eight Sleep cooling mattress).
6. **Reframe a boring product into a new category** (issue #157): baby carrots became ~80% of carrot consumption by being repositioned as a grab-and-go snack competing with junk food (+10% sales via indulgence framing).

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Only pick a fight you can win, attacking a rival that outsells you badly can backfire as negative social proof (see the `comparison-copy` copywriting card).
- The enemy gives customers a side to join; the Onlyness statement gives them the reason; the trade-off makes it defensible.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: given a product + its category, propose 2-3 candidate "enemies," draft an Onlyness statement, and suggest a contrarian trade-off axis (what to be deliberately worse at) to own a distinct position.

### Earn attention with stunts and reactive moments, but run the impressions math first

## The strategy
Guerrilla stunts, reactive social moments, and self-aware transparency can generate outsized earned reach, but only if the payoff beats what the same money would buy in paid media. Estimate required impressions before you build anything.

## When to use it
Brand-awareness pushes on a tight budget, launch moments, viral-trend opportunities, and reactive PR (someone's talking about you right now).

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Do the earned-media ROI math first** (issue #247): estimate the impressions a stunt must earn to beat your paid CPMs. Poppi's 32 pink vending machines (~$25K each, ~$800K total) needed 80-100M impressions to beat $8-10 beverage CPMs. If the stunt can't plausibly hit that, it's a paid campaign in disguise.
2. **Get bold hooks at any budget** (issue #247): Surreal Cereal hired everyday people who happen to *share celebrity names* for cheap "celebrity endorsement" billboards.
3. **Make stunts that telegraph the product** (issue #273): Mischief's fake prototypes ("TAXIDER-MY-FAMILY"); use named à-la-carte configuration vignettes to reduce cognitive load.
4. **React in real time to earn reach** (issue #149): Stanley's founder posted a 30-second selfie reply within 24h of the viral car-fire post → 38M views. North Face's helicopter-delivery response (5 days) → 4M views. Speed multiplies reach.
5. **Join viral trends about you and lead with radical transparency** (issue #271): Jet2 ran a £1,000 lip-sync contest on its own meme audio (2.8M videos) → 34M+ views; Domino's 2009 "our crust tastes like cardboard" self-critique → 14.3% Q1 2010 sales jump (stock $8.38 → $564.33 by 2021); Hans Brinker owned "chaotic budget hostel." Honest specificity self-filters for aligned customers.
6. **Consider intentional "mistake"/limited-deal framing for buzz** (issue #115), inspired by Ken Davenport's Broadway ticket strategy, drives word-of-mouth without giveaways.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The math gate is the whole discipline, most "viral stunt" ideas quietly lose to boring paid ads on a cost-per-impression basis.
- Reactive speed compounds reach: a same-day response dramatically outperforms a polished one that lands a week later.
- Radical transparency works because it's disarming and self-qualifying; don't fake the flaw you're owning.

## → Skill conversion note
Partial skill: the impressions-vs-CPM calculator is automatable (inputs: stunt cost + target CPM → required impressions to break even). The creative side stays human, guided by the reactive/transparency examples.

### Rebrand without torching equity, evolve distinctive assets, don't wholesale-replace them

## The strategy
Most rebrands destroy value by discarding the very assets customers use to recognize the brand. The rule: evolve distinctive assets incrementally, keep serving your core audience, and only modernize what's genuinely dated.

## When to use it
Any logo/identity refresh, a rebrand debate, modernizing a legacy brand, or resisting a "blow it all up" redesign push.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Evolve, don't replace, distinctive brand assets** (issue #266), the colors, shapes, and marks customers recognize. Wholesale swaps reset recognition to zero.
2. **Avoid the Jaguar anti-patterns** (issue #225): don't abandon your core audience to chase a new demographic; launch rebrand campaigns *alongside an actual product*, not into a product vacuum; and don't use ironic slogans ("Copy Nothing") that contradict your real strategy.
3. **Audit logo legibility under 200px** (issue #274): check favicons, app icons, and thumbnails. If detail disappears at small sizes, simplify.
4. **Apply the five modernization traits** where warranted (issue #274): 1-2 hues, no 3D/shadows, wordmark over symbol, strip serifs, add breathing room. Expect 4-12 weeks of backlash before judging results.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Cautionary numbers: Tropicana lost $30M+ in 60 days and reverted; Jaguar −97.5% YoY after its 2024 rebrand; JCPenney lost $4B in a year (bankrupt 2020); HBO Max → Max lost premium equity and reverted.
- Cracker Barrel context (issue #274): 43% of customers are 55+, and foodservice transactions fell 7% YoY in Q1 2025, a reminder to know who your rebrand risks alienating.
- Backlash is expected and temporary, but only judge after the 4-12 week window, and only if you protected recognition and your core audience.

## → Skill conversion note
Weak skill candidate, more a decision checklist than an automatable tool. Best as a "rebrand risk audit" guide: evaluate a proposed rebrand against the evolve-don't-replace, core-audience, and legibility criteria.

### Audit your brand on two axes, warmth and competence (and invert them for luxury)

## The strategy
People judge brands the way they judge people: on **warmth** (does it have good intentions toward me?) and **competence** (can it deliver?). These two judgments explain roughly half of purchase intent, loyalty, and likelihood to recommend, so audit and deliberately signal both.

## When to use it
Brand perception audits, customer-experience design, trust-building for a new brand, and repositioning a brand that's seen as either cold-but-capable or nice-but-flaky.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Score your brand on both axes** (issue #103, Fiske & Malone research):
   - **Warmth** signals: comped products, handwritten thank-you notes, patient/generous support. Zappos' famous 9.5-hour support call + early shoe delivery.
   - **Competence** signals: deliver reliably, on time, as promised.
2. **Find your weak axis and add concrete proof** for it, warmth is shown through generous human gestures; competence through reliability and results.
3. **For luxury/aspirational brands, invert it** (issue #103): prioritize competence over warmth. A little rejection from an aspirational brand *increases* desire, lean on exclusivity and status, not friendliness.
4. **Boost warmth via the Unity principle** (issue #131): create affinity through a founder story matched to the audience's pain, values alignment (Patagonia), named community labels (Lady Gaga's "Little Monsters"), and hometown/shared identity.
5. **Emphasize "groundedness"** (issue #119), place, people, and past: local origin, traditional design, founder stories, local distribution. Remote workers and urban dwellers show ~60% higher willingness-to-pay for "grounded" products.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The two axes are a fast diagnostic: most brands are strong on one and blind to the other.
- Warmth without competence reads as charming but unreliable; competence without warmth reads as capable but cold, either gap caps loyalty.
- Luxury is the deliberate exception: warmth can *cheapen* an aspirational brand.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: a checklist tool that rates a brand's current touchpoints on warmth vs. competence, flags the weaker axis, and suggests concrete signals (Unity/groundedness tactics for warmth, reliability proof for competence).

### When everybody zigs, zag, break one category norm to build a distinctive brand

## The strategy
In a category where every brand uses the same colors, packaging, and messaging, sameness makes you invisible. The move is to audit the category's universal signals and deliberately invert one signature element so you become the distinctive (and therefore memorable) option.

## When to use it
Crowded consumer categories, commodity products, any market where competitors are visually interchangeable, and packaging/identity decisions.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Audit the category's universal patterns** (issue #249): the default color, layout, tone, and packaging everyone uses. Pick ONE signature element and deliberately invert it, don't change everything, change the one thing people will notice.
2. **Break a category norm as a pattern interrupt** (issue #106): Pringles' tube vs. rectangular bags; Liquid Death's hardcore metal look in minimalist water; SparkToro's 30-min, no-recording events.
3. **Design un-ignorable, category-different packaging** (issue #097): Sundays cookies look like "a children's book"; high saturation vs. Oreo/Chips Ahoy; Liquid Death, Smart Sweets.
4. **Apply the Von Restorff (isolation) effect** (issue #134): the different thing gets remembered. Standout packaging, a contrarian identity, or showing competitors muted while you're in full color.
5. **Match distinctiveness effort to category interest via the "YouTube test"** (issue #193): if creators make content about your category (cars), go rich and narrative; if none do (toilet paper), pick ONE simple distinctive angle, Oatly absurdity, Who Gives A Crap humor, Dude Wipes puns.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Real outcomes (issue #249): Van Leeuwen +50% sales after its 2017 redesign; Graza's neon squeeze bottles → $240M valuation; Liquid Death cans → $1.4B; Notion's minimalist B&W → $10B.
- Invert *one* element. Inverting everything creates chaos, not distinctiveness, the brand needs a recognizable anchor.
- Distinctiveness ≠ good taste; it's about being *different from the set*, then being consistent enough to build memory (see `mere-exposure-effect`).

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: input a category + 3-5 competitor brands, auto-extract the shared visual/messaging conventions, and propose which single element to invert (with examples of brands that zagged the same way).
