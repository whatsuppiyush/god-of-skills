# AI Photography: The DSLR Style Guide

Most AI images look fake for one reason: they never name how a real photograph is made. Real photographers choose a camera body, a lens, a film stock, an angle and a light, and that language carries into the image. This guide is that language, distilled from a working photography and cinematography vocabulary and tested against current models. It runs long on purpose: work through it top to bottom the first time, then come back and use the table of contents like an index.

Works in: Midjourney, GPT Image, Flux, Nano Banana, Stable Diffusion.

## How to use this skill

When the user asks for an image prompt in this style, compose one using the formulas and vocabulary below. Fill each slot with a concrete choice, then return the finished prompt. Prefer naming a real camera/lens, a light, and a colour or film treatment, which is what makes the output read as a real photograph rather than an AI render.

## Formulas

**The photograph formula**
```
[shot type] of [subject + action], [setting], shot on [camera + lens], [lighting], [film stock / colour grade], ultra-realistic, natural skin texture
```
The order matters less than the ingredients. Every slot you fill pulls the image further from 'AI render' toward 'photograph'. Naming a real camera and lens is the single biggest jump in realism.

**The cinematic-still formula**
```
Cinematic film still from a [year] [genre] film, [shot type] of [subject + action], [setting], directed by [director], shot on [cinema camera], [lighting], [emotion], cinematic colour grading, film grain
```
Starting with 'cinematic film still' tells the model to render a frame from a real movie, not a poster. Naming a director tints scenery, wardrobe and mood; adding a decade adds period detail.

### Angles & shot types

**Distance & framing**
- **Extreme long shot**: Great distance from the subject, who is barely visible. Used to highlight surroundings, scale and location over the subject itself.
- **Long shot (wide shot)**: Shows the entire subject and their environment. For a person, this means the full body with real surrounding context.
- **Medium shot**: Frames roughly waist-up or chest-up. The default for dialogue scenes: enough face to read emotion, enough of the room to place them in it.
- **Closeup shot**: The subject, usually a face, fills the frame. Emphasises detail and emotion over context.
- **Extreme closeup shot**: Isolates one feature, an eye, a hand, a mouth. Emphasises a single specific detail intensely.
- **Full shot**: Head to toe, the entire body visible in the scene. Showcases physicality, posture and action.
- **Two-shot**: Frames two subjects together. The standard for conversation scenes; it establishes the relationship between them.

**Angle & perspective**
- **Over-the-shoulder shot**: Shot from behind one subject, their shoulder and the back of their head in frame. Adds depth and intimacy to dialogue.
- **Point-of-view (POV) shot**: Frames the scene exactly as the subject would see it, putting the viewer inside their perspective.
- **Establishing shot**: Usually an extreme long shot that opens a scene, telling the viewer where and when the action is happening.
- **Master shot**: Includes all key figures and the full action of a scene. Often the first shot filmed or shown in a sequence.
- **Low-angle shot**: Camera positioned below the subject, looking up. Makes the subject appear larger, more dominant, more powerful.
- **High-angle shot**: Camera positioned above the subject, looking down. Makes the subject appear smaller, less significant, sometimes vulnerable.
- **Eye-level shot**: Camera at the subject's own eye level. Neutral and natural: how you'd actually see them in person.
- **Dutch angle shot**: The camera is intentionally tilted off the horizontal. Reads as disorientation, tension or unease.
- **Overhead shot**: Camera directly above the subject, looking straight down.
- **Bird's-eye view shot**: Higher than an overhead shot, as if viewed from the sky. Emphasises scale and a wide perspective.
- **Drone shot**: An aerial shot captured via drone, typically higher and more mobile than a bird's-eye or overhead shot.
- **Worm's-eye view**: Looking up at the subject from ground level. Dramatic, and makes the subject feel formidable.
- **Ground-level view**: Camera positioned at ground level, with the background rising up from the horizon line.
- **Side view**: Camera positioned to the side of the subject rather than facing them directly.
- **Off-centre view**: The subject is placed away from the centre of the frame, adding visual interest and tension.
- **Rule-of-thirds shot**: The frame is divided into nine equal parts, with the subject placed along the grid lines. The default for balanced, professional composition.

**Style & treatment**
- **Candid shot**: Taken as if the subject doesn't know they're being photographed. Reads as natural and relaxed, the opposite of a posed portrait.
- **Silhouette shot**: The subject is backlit and the exposure is set for the background, so the subject renders as a dark shape.
- **Shot from behind**: Frames the subject from their back. Dramatic and mysterious: it withholds the face.
- **Underwater shot**: Captured from beneath the water's surface, for perspectives you can't get any other way.

### Lighting

**Natural lighting**
- **Sunlight**: The most common outdoor light source. Its quality shifts everything else: hard and direct at noon, soft and warm at the edges of the day.
- **Moonlight**: Soft, cool, ethereal. Reads as night without going fully dark.
- **Firelight / candlelight**: Warm, flickering, intimate. The default for cozy, rustic or romantic indoor scenes.
- **Cloudy skies / overcast**: Soft, diffused, shadowless light. The most flattering condition for portraits.
- **Reflections**: Shimmering light bounced off water, glass or wet surfaces. Pairs well with other lighting for a dreamy effect.

**Ambient lighting**
- **Overhead**: Ceiling lights or chandeliers, the default indoor ambient source.
- **Wall sconces**: Decorative fixtures that throw soft, indirect light.
- **Table lamps**: Can read as warm and cozy, or bright and harsh, depending on the shade and bulb.
- **Floor lamps**: Warm and cozy, useful for lighting the corners of a room.
- **Natural light**: Light entering through windows or skylights. Gives a scene consistent, believable illumination.

**Artificial & directional lighting**
- **Studio lights**: Fully controlled light. The default for portrait, product and fashion work.
- **Strobe lights**: A bright flash. Freezes motion and adds punch.
- **Ring lights**: Even, flattering, shadow-minimising light. The beauty-portrait and influencer-video default.
- **Neon lights**: Coloured, saturated light. The signature of a cyberpunk or night-city look.
- **LED lights**: A wide range of colour temperatures, from clinical cool to warm practical.
- **Butterfly lighting**: Light placed above and slightly in front of the face, casting a small shadow under the nose. A classic glamour setup.
- **Rembrandt lighting**: A triangular patch of light on the cheek under one eye, with dramatic shadow on the rest of the face.
- **Split lighting**: Light placed at 90 degrees to the face, so exactly half is lit and half is in shadow.
- **Rim lighting**: Placed behind the subject to illuminate their edges, separating them from the background. Moody and cinematic.
- **Low-key lighting**: One or two light sources, mostly shadow. Dramatic and moody: the default for noir and horror.
- **High-key lighting**: Bright, even, mostly shadow-free. Light and airy: the default for clean, commercial images.
- **Top light**: Light from directly above, emphasising texture and shape through hard shadows.
- **Side light**: Strong shadows and highlights from one side, emphasising texture and dimension.
- **Backlighting**: Light placed behind the subject, creating a glowing halo around them.

**Hard & soft lighting**
- **Direct sunlight**: Strong, well-defined shadows. Punchy but unforgiving.
- **Spot lighting**: A tight, strong beam. Creates sharp shadows and highlights.
- **Halogen bulbs**: Harsh, intense light, associated with commercial and industrial spaces.
- **Laser lighting**: Sharp coloured beams. For stage and concert effects.
- **Fresnel lighting**: A focusing lens used in theatre and film lighting rigs.
- **Hard fill lighting**: A second light used to fill in shadows without softening them entirely. Common in commercial and fashion work.
- **Window light**: Soft and directional. The classic indoor portrait light.
- **Cloudy days**: Naturally soft, even light with no hard shadows.
- **Softbox lighting**: Softens and evenly distributes a studio light source.
- **Umbrella lighting**: Reflects and diffuses light for a soft, even result.
- **Beauty dish lighting**: Soft but still directional. A flattering middle ground for portraits.

### Colour

- **Hexachromatic**: Six colours.

- **Polychromatic**: Effectively infinite: full rainbow range.
- **Analogous colors**: Colours adjacent on the colour wheel.
- **Triadic colors**: Three colours evenly spaced around the wheel.

**Extended colours**
- **Tan**: A warm, muted light brown.
- **Beige**: A pale, neutral sand tone.
- **Blush**: A soft pink.
- **Scarlet**: A vibrant red.
- **Olive-green**: A muted, earthy green.

### Theme: realism & abstraction

**Realism & abstraction**
- **Hyper real / hyperrealistic**: Pushes past normal photographic realism. Pairs well with horror and caricature.
- **Magic realism**: The mundane world with one impossible element treated as normal. Pairs well with dream and fantasy scenes.
- **Fantastic realism**: Realistic rendering of fantastical subjects. Pairs well with dream and horror scenes.
- **Contemporary realism / new realism**: Grounded, present-day realism. Pairs well with people and dramatic scenes.
- **Surreal / surrealism**: Dreamlike, illogical juxtapositions. Pairs well with weird and creepy scenes.

- **Perforated**: Punctured with regular holes.

**Hardness & phase**
- **Soft / hard**: The basic material-firmness axis.
- **Soft body / squishy**: Deformable, yielding.

- **Melting / freezing**: Mid-transition between liquid and solid.

**Reflections & refractions**
- **Rough / matte**: Diffuse, non-reflective surface.
- **Glossy / shiny / polished**: Smooth, reflective surface.
- **Reflection / reflective / retroreflective**: Bounces light back toward its source.
- **Refraction / refractive**: Bends light passing through the material.
- **Caustics**: Focused light patterns cast by refraction, like light through water.
- **Glare / specular highlights**: Bright, concentrated points of reflected light.
- **Shimmer / shimmering / glimmering**: Fine, moving highlights across a surface.

**Optics & light manipulation**
- **Transparent / translucent / opaque**: How much light passes through the material.
- **Scattering / subsurface-scattering**: Light diffusing beneath a surface, as in skin or wax.
- **Ambient occlusion**: Soft shadowing in crevices and contact points.
- **Opalescent**: Milky, shifting iridescence.
- **Polarized / polarization**: Light filtered to a single orientation.
- **Solarized / solarization**: A partial tonal inversion, a classic darkroom effect.
- **Iridescent**: Colour that shifts with viewing angle.
- **Dispersion / chromatic / prismatic**: Light splitting into its component colours.

- **Cherenkov radiation**: The eerie blue glow associated with high-speed particles, useful for sci-fi.
- **Luminescence / bioluminescence**: Light generated by a living organism, think a glowing forest.
- **Chromism**: Colour that changes in response to a stimulus.
- **Piezochromism**: Colour change from pressure.
- **Tribochromism**: Colour change from friction, like race-car tyres.
- **Metallochromism**: Colour change from metal content.
- **Goniochromism**: Colour that shifts with viewing angle.
- **Hydrochromism**: Colour change from water exposure, like a mug that reveals an image when filled.
