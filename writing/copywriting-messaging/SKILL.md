# Copywriting & Messaging

A working system for persuasive marketing copy: research the customer's own
words, pick the right structural framework, sharpen the headline, ladder features
into benefits the buyer actually cares about, differentiate against alternatives,
and pass a quality gate before shipping. The tactics live in `references/`; this
file routes you to the right one and shows how they fit together.

## When to use this

Reach for this whenever the words that sell are the problem:

- Writing a headline, subject line, hero H1, tagline, or ad hook.
- Rewriting a homepage, landing page, product description, or About page.
- "Our messaging isn't landing / converts poorly / sounds generic."
- A value prop any competitor could also claim (undifferentiated positioning).
- Features that read like a spec sheet instead of a reason to buy.
- Prospects hesitating at pricing/checkout (objections, missing proof).
- An ad that gets scrolled past in the first 2 seconds.
- Copy that "sounds like AI" or like the founder wrote it, not the buyer.
- A final polish pass on CTAs, prices, reviews, and offers.

## When NOT to use this (reach for instead)

- **Why a message persuades at all** (cognitive biases, emotional drivers, pricing
  psychology, the mental models behind these tactics): use `marketing-psychology`.
- **Brand-level identity, voice archetype, naming, and visual system** beyond the
  copy on the page: use `brand`. (Story Systems below hands off to it once the
  narrative kernel is set.)
- **Where the copy runs and whether that channel is right** (paid vs organic,
  channel strategy, budget): use the relevant channel skill, not this one.
- **Cold outbound copy to strangers** (cold email/DM sequences): use
  `sales-outbound`. This skill assumes a reader who already has context (a page
  visitor, an opted-in subscriber, an ad audience).

## How this works (decision path)

Copy fails for one of three reasons. Diagnose first, then pull the matching play:

1. **Wrong problem / undifferentiated (strategy).** If the copy describes the
   product accurately but still converts poorly, the issue is usually upstream of
   the words. Start with **positioning & reframe**: run the Swap Test, reframe the
   category problem, and check the market's sophistication stage. Fix this before
   touching a single headline.
2. **Right problem, weak expression (craft).** Once you know what to say: mine the
   **voice-of-customer** language, choose a **framework** to structure it, ladder
   **features into benefits**, sharpen the **headline**, and win by **contrast**.
3. **Right expression, unpolished (finish).** Apply **micro-copy** swaps, then run
   the **quality gates** before shipping.

Feeder step: nearly every play consumes mined customer language, so run
`voice-of-customer-copy-research.md` early. It is the research layer that feeds
positioning, benefits, objections, and headlines.

## The plays

### Positioning & reframe (fix this first)
- **Make your value prop unstealable (Swap Test + Dunford process)**
- **Reframe the category's problem instead of restating it (Reframe Ladder, Rule of One)**
- **Match the message to the market's sophistication stage (Schwartz's 5 stages)**

### Voice-of-customer research (the feeder layer)
- **Write from mined customer language (S.C.A.R.F. research, 10-angle matrix)**
- **Preempt objections early and back claims with mined social proof**

### Frameworks & story structure
- **Structure copy with BAB, PAS, or AIDA**
- **Sell with the hero's-journey arc, sensory detail, and real-people voice**
- **Sequence a story around tension, not chronology (open on conflict, open loops)**
- **Make ads unignorable (fourth-wall breaks, juxtaposition, rhyme)**
- **Build one coherent brand narrative (Story Systems, 7 parts)**

### Headlines
- **Generate headlines by volume, then cull (Mullet/Smile, "wait what?", speed drills)**
- **Score headlines against the Four U's, rhyme, power words, specificity**

### Benefit laddering
- **Translate every feature into a benefit and an emotional real-life value**
- **Ladder past the obvious benefit (the "benefit of the benefit", the "so what?" chain)**

### Comparison & contrast
- **Win by contrast (weak-alternative framing, persona comparison, challenger tactics)**
- **Confess a weakness to sell a strength (admit trade-offs, invert a constraint)**

### Micro-copy (final polish)
- **High-lift word-level swaps ("because" reasons, oddly-specific numbers, present tense, conditionals)**

### Quality gates (before you ship)
- **The 3-question test (visualize / falsify / nobody-else) plus 8 AI "tells" to delete**

## Key numbers & benchmarks

- The headline earns ~80% of an ad's attention: about 5x as many people read the
  headline as read the body. Specificity is the highest-leverage edit, so swap
  every vague superlative for a real number.
- A conditional headline using "may" beat "will" by 200%. Softening absolutes also
  lowers overpromise risk.
- Adding a "because" reason to a request lifted compliance from 60% to 94%.
- Oddly-specific numbers beat round ones: specific-amount asks drew about 60% more
  than round-number ones.
- Present-tense reviews earned 26.4% more upvotes and +12.3% purchase intent vs.
  past tense.
- Swapping one vague CTA word for a concrete one ("your site" to "your homepage")
  doubled homepage conversion in one test.
- Positioning-only rewrites moved real numbers: one SaaS doubled homepage
  conversions and lifted ad CTR +22%; another added +30% from messaging alone.
- A classic headline that passed the 3-question test lifted sales 50 to 100% on
  about 2% of a rival's ad budget.
- Rule of three: three items is the minimum for a pattern a reader latches onto.
- Benefit-frequency in reviews is the cheapest way to rank your angles: the market
  has already voted on what matters.

### Generate headlines by volume, then cull, Mullet/Smile, "wait what?" formulas, and a 6-technique speed process

## The strategy
Great headlines are a numbers game: generate a large pile fast, throw the bad ones away, refine the survivors. This card is the generation engine (not the scoring criteria, that's the Four U's card). It bundles repeatable formulas and speed drills for producing dozens of candidate headlines, then narrowing.

## When to use it
Ad headlines, landing-page headers, subject lines, social hooks, taglines, any time you're staring at a blank line and want more shots on goal than your first idea.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Six-technique speed drill (≤15 min):** (1) state the plain factual message first as a fallback; (2) the **Mullet / "Smile"** = serious factual value prop up front + a joke or playful twist in back; (3) remix a common phrase ("Don't sweat the small stuff" → "…sweaty stuff" for deodorant); (4) Opposites ("Small rates. Big deal."); (5) "100 MPH writing", 15-min timer, zero judgment, dump everything; (6) bucket-fill (list 3 value props → benefit scenarios → headlines).
2. **"Wait, what?" formulas** that force a double-take: contradiction, abnormal specificity, deadpan honesty, reverse logic, curiosity gap. E.g. "Double your revenue by deleting half of your website"; "It took 19 tries to make a cookie our grandma wouldn't hate."
3. **Dan Nelken's 3-step volume process:** list 20+ "idea buckets" (benefits, attributes, truths, too-obvious is fine); force 10 first-thought ideas per bucket; craft the survivors into headlines. Mine competitors, FAQs, reviews, and socials for raw material.
4. **7-prompt ChatGPT sequence** for a first pile: value props → exclusiveness → "Get"-led headlines → customer goal → goal-achievement framing → urgency layer → "give me 10 examples of this concept."
5. **Cull:** score every candidate against 10 emotional hooks, humor (LOL), surprise (WTF), delight, clarity, validation (FINALLY!), celebration, fear, aspiration, curiosity, credibility. Keep the ones that land a hook; kill the flat ones.
6. For taglines specifically: generate 200+ options → narrow to 5-10 → stress-test in the actual logo lockup / ad creative → validate with real customers.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The Mullet framework is one of the most reliable headline moves: clarity + emotion + memorability in one line.
- Core principle across all of it: "get lots of ideas, throw the bad ones away." Volume before refinement, don't polish your first draft.
- AI struggles with novelty, so use ChatGPT to *generate quantity and cull clichés*, not to pick the winner.

## → Skill conversion note
Strong skill: input product + 3 value props, run the 7-prompt sequence and 6-technique drill to output 30-50 candidate headlines, then auto-score each against the 10 emotional hooks and return a ranked shortlist.

### Ladder features past the obvious benefit, the "benefit of the benefit" and "so what?" to a Maslow need

## The strategy
The basic feature→benefit move stops one rung too early. The real decision-driver is usually the *benefit of the benefit*, the second-order payoff the buyer actually cares about. This card extends the features-to-benefits card with the advanced laddering techniques.

## When to use it
When your benefits sound generic ("saves time," "easy to use"), when you're selling to one person but the payoff matters to another (parents buy for kids), or when reframing a commodity value prop.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **The "benefit of the benefit" exercise** (issue #199): list the benefits AND the downsides of your product, then ask "what's an unexpected benefit of *this* benefit?", for the real decision-maker. Springfree Trampoline: kids' fun → parents' peace of mind. Jeep off-road capability → "park anywhere." McDonald's 24/7 → "good at interpreting drunk people at 2am." (Resource: Dan Nelken's copywriting guide.)
2. **Dimensionalize a feature with "so what?"** down to a Maslow need (issue #108): XL art print → focal point of the room → conversation piece → social approval → esteem. Keep asking "so what?" until you hit an emotional/identity need.
3. **Reframe the generic "time savings" value prop** (issue #162): translate saved time into (a) lower costs (headcount/freelancer spend), (b) higher revenue (shorter sales cycles, faster GTM), or (c) life quality. Calendly = "more deals closed"; Zapier = "do what you do best."
4. **Sell instant gratification, not just the future benefit** (issue #106): add the immediate emotional payoff, "Order now and you'll feel relief knowing you made the right decision." Disney frames trip-*planning* itself as part of the reward.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Complements the existing `features-to-benefits` card, that one gets you to the first benefit; this one gets you to the one that actually converts.
- The trick with "so what?" is to stop at the emotionally resonant rung, not to over-abstract into vagueness.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: input a feature and the buyer/decision-maker, then auto-run the "so what?" ladder and "benefit of the benefit" prompt to output 3-5 second-order benefit angles mapped to the payoff that person cares about.

### Win by contrast, weak-alternative comparisons, indirect persona framing, and challenger-vs-incumbent tactics

## The strategy
Value is relative. Copy that shows your product next to a deliberately weak or contrasting alternative reads as obviously better, because people judge "what you see is all there is" (WYSIATI). This card is the comparison-copy toolkit: which contrast to pick and how to frame it.

## When to use it
Ads and landing pages where the buyer doesn't yet feel your product's advantage; challenger brands positioning against an incumbent; categories where the real competitor is an old habit or a clunky alternative.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Frame against a weak alternative** (issue #191, WYSIATI): pick the comparison that flatters you. Loop earplugs vs. foam, Huel vs. instant noodles, Healthycell vs. "the entire pharmacy." Four reusable templates: **product-vs-nothing**, **before/after**, **want-vs-reality**, **group A vs. group B**.
2. **Compare personas/worldviews, not specs** (indirect comparison): Apple's "Get a Mac" contrasted *types of user* rather than feature lists, the audience self-sorts into the flattering side.
3. **Challenger-vs-incumbent tactics** (issue #219): the secret wink (Surreal), explicit X-vs-Y factual claims, fact-first (McDonald's drive-thru density), invert their branding (Huel stripping AG1's styling), stereotype humor (Mac vs. PC).
4. **Only pick a fight you can win** and won't get sued over. Bashing a rival that outsells you 4x backfires, it becomes negative social proof, signaling *they're* the default choice.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- WYSIATI means the reader compares only against what's *on screen*. Control the frame and you control the verdict.
- Indirect persona comparison sidesteps direct-attack legal risk while still forcing a choice.
- Pair with the `flaw-reframing` card (admit a trade-off) to keep comparison copy credible rather than boastful.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: input your product + its real alternatives, then output comparison-copy options across the four templates (product-vs-nothing / before-after / want-vs-reality / A-vs-B), with a warning flag if the chosen rival is a stronger-selling incumbent.

### Pre-publish copy gates, the 3-question test (visualize / falsify / nobody-else) and 8 AI "tells" to delete

## The strategy
Two review checklists that run *after* drafting: one raises the ceiling (is this line vivid and ownable?), one raises the floor (does it read like AI slop?). Together they're a quality gate before anything ships.

## When to use it
Final pass on any headline, tagline, or key line of copy; reviewing AI-generated drafts; a standing editorial checklist for a content team.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **The three-question copy test** (issue #202), rewrite until you get three yeses:
   - **Can you visualize it?** ("Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio"; iPod "1,000 songs in your pocket.")
   - **Can you falsify it?** A concrete, checkable claim beats a vague one (Volvo's odometer proof, Heinz's thickness demo).
   - **Can nobody else say it?** If a competitor could paste it verbatim, it's not yours (this is the Swap Test at the line level).
2. **Delete the 8 AI "tells"** (issue #268): "not just X, it's Y"; em dashes; hedging; forced triplets ("simple, smart, seamless"); grandiose verbs (unlock/leverage/harness); rhetorical-question transitions; unverified "studies show"; metaphorical "tapestry."
3. Ship only after both gates pass. If a line fails the 3-question test, it's weak; if it trips the AI tells, it's generic, either way, rewrite.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The three-question test is Harry Dry's (Marketing Examples). A classic Rolls-Royce ad that passed it lifted sales 50-100% on a $50k budget, about 2% of Cadillac's spend.
- The AI-tells list doubles as a house-style banned-words list, useful for godofskills' own copy, where em-dashes and "unlock/leverage/transform" are already banned.
- Falsifiability is the sleeper: specific, provable claims outperform superlatives precisely because they invite scrutiny.

## → Skill conversion note
Strong skill: paste a line of copy and get a pass/fail on each of the three questions plus a highlighted list of any AI tells present, with concrete rewrite suggestions.

### Structure persuasive copy with BAB, PAS, or AIDA

## The strategy
Three interchangeable skeletons for persuasive copy. Pick one based on how emotionally charged the problem is, then fill each beat.

## When to use it
Landing pages, ads, email, and any copy that needs to move a reader from awareness to action. BAB for aspirational transformation, PAS when pain is acute enough to agitate, AIDA for ad/short-form flow.

## How to execute (steps)
**BAB, Before / After / Bridge**
1. Before: name the problem/pain.
2. After: show life once it's solved.
3. Bridge: your product is how they get there.
- Porsche example: driving stuck behind another car (before) → gripping the sport wheel (after) → buy a Porsche (bridge).

**PAS, Problem / Agitate / Solve**
1. Problem: identify the pain.
2. Agitate: heighten the negative emotion (frustration, fear, anger).
3. Solve: present the product as resolution.
- Example: Ferry Porsche couldn't find his dream car (problem) → frustration with inferior cars (agitate) → he built his own (solve).

**AIDA, Attention / Interest / Desire / Action**
1. Attention: grab with a headline or image.
2. Interest: build intrigue with compelling detail.
3. Desire: make them want the outcome.
4. Action: one clear next step.
- Example: "400 horses. Zero to sixty in just over four heartbeats" (interest) → "Call 1-800-Porsche" (action).

## Notes / caveats / examples
- These aren't mutually exclusive, a page can nest a PAS hook inside an AIDA arc.
- Choose PAS only when the pain is real enough to agitate without feeling manipulative.

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes a `copy-framework-fill` prompt: input product + audience pain, output drafts in all three frameworks so the user can pick the strongest fit.

### Translate every feature into a benefit and an emotional real-life value

## The strategy
"People don't buy products; they buy better versions of themselves." Spotlight outcomes, not specs. For every feature, ladder it up to a benefit and then to an emotional, real-life value.

## When to use it
Product descriptions, landing-page feature sections, ad body copy, sales pages.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Run the feature → benefit → value ladder** for each feature:
   - Feature: what it is (wide-mouth water bottle).
   - Benefit: what it does (faster refills).
   - Value (emotional): what that means for their life ("spend less time at the dispenser, more time running or hiking").
2. **Write product descriptions that answer:** What's in it? How do I use it? What's the experience?
   - Use vivid, sensory language matched to the audience.
   - Bullet the benefits for scannability.
   - Make only real claims (no exaggeration).
   - Add purchasing scenarios ("perfect gift").
3. **Use sensory, vivid language**, paint with sight/taste/touch/sound/smell. Lush: "shimmering fizzer melts into a sunset of floral and gold." Cheetos: bright orange fingertips (visual + tactile).
4. **Use action-oriented verbs** in headers and CTAs: find, get, make, catch, relax, feel, wake up (Headspace uses all-verb copy).
5. **Add interactivity** where possible: adjustable parameters (Give Directly: change donation, see lives impacted), feature checkboxes (Monday.com), comparison tables, tabbed/expandable sections (Lush: ingredients / how to use / FAQs).

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Lush product page = vivid sensory copy + benefit focus + tabbed FAQs + bulleted list, all in one.
- Interactivity increases engagement and dwell time on the page.

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes a `feature-to-benefit` prompt: paste a feature list, get back a benefit + emotional-value line for each, plus a scannable bulleted product description.

### Confess a weakness to sell a strength, admit trade-offs and invert a constraint into a positioning asset

## The strategy
Naming a real flaw makes every other claim believable, and it self-qualifies buyers so the right ones lean in. Better still: take the constraint you'd normally hide and flip it into the reason to buy.

## When to use it
High-skepticism categories, premium products fighting "too good to be true," commoditized markets where you can own a deliberate trade-off, and any time generic superlatives ("best-in-class") are being ignored.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Admit a genuine trade-off** (issue #203): being candid about a weakness makes your strength claims credible and filters out mismatched buyers. Audit your product's real limitations and name a *specific* trade-off, not a generic disclaimer. VW called its cars "ugly"/"slow" to sell reliability; SAAB said "nobody's dream car."
2. **Invert a constraint into an asset** (issue #207): name what you're NOT → list the downside honestly → flip it to a benefit. Guinness (slow pour → "good things come to those who wait"); Stella ("reassuringly expensive"); Buckley's ("tastes awful but it works"); Avis ("we're #2, so we try harder"); Nintendo Switch (underpowered but portable).
3. Choose the ONE trade-off you're happy to be known for, and make it consistent across homepage, ads, and sales copy so it reads as conviction, not apology.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The flaw must be *real and specific*, a fake or trivial "weakness" ("we care too much") reads as manipulation.
- Works because it violates the expectation that ads only flatter, the honesty itself is the pattern interrupt.
- Pairs with the `comparison-copy` card: an admitted trade-off keeps contrast copy from tipping into unbelievable bragging.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: input a product's honest weaknesses, then output "confession" copy lines (VW/SAAB style) and constraint-inversion angles (name-what-you're-not → downside → flipped benefit) for the buyer to pick from.

### Make ads unignorable, fourth-wall breaks, juxtaposition (absurd + plain), and rhyme

## The strategy
Three attention devices that survive the scroll by breaking the pattern of a "normal ad": talk to the viewer as a co-conspirator (fourth wall), pair one absurd element with one plain one (juxtaposition), or make the line rhyme so it sticks.

## When to use it
Paid social and video ads where the first 2 seconds decide everything; brand campaigns that need to feel human rather than corporate; any placement fighting ad-blindness.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Two fourth-wall techniques** (issue #213): (a) speak directly to camera as a co-conspirator (Dollar Shave Club, Old Spice); (b) openly acknowledge "this is an ad" with humor (Oatly, Surreal, RxBar, L'Oréal). Lewis Capaldi's ad shows the full arc: hook → re-hook with credibility → self-deprecate to avoid bragging → "you/I" fourth-wall break → make it shareable.
2. **The juxtaposition framework** (issue #204): pair exactly one absurd element with one plain one, either a "twisted visual + straight line" (exaggerated image, boring copy) or a "twisted line + straight visual." Bob Gill's rule: "Interesting words need boring graphics." The contrast controls the attention → scan → payoff sequence. Examples: Fiver's AI-job ad, Porsche, Nicotinell.
3. **A/B test rhyming copy** (issue #086) in ads, subject lines, and CTAs, rhymes read as more likable, memorable, and trustworthy ("rhyme-as-reason" effect). "Zapier makes you happier"; Headspace's "Be kind to your mind."

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Juxtaposition's power is *restraint*: two absurd elements cancel out into noise; one absurd + one plain creates the payoff sequence.
- Fourth-wall/ad-acknowledgment works best for brands with permission to be playful, it can undercut a premium/serious positioning.
- These are attention devices; pair them with a real value prop or you win the scroll and lose the sale.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: given an ad concept, generate a fourth-wall "co-conspirator" script variant, a juxtaposition (absurd-visual + plain-line) variant, and 3 rhyming headline options to A/B test.

### Write headlines that are Unique, Urgent, Ultra-specific, and Useful, then sharpen with rhyme, power words, and specificity

## The strategy
The headline does most of the work, Ogilvy: "five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy... you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar." The rules for making it earn that spend: the Four U's, rhyme, power words, ruthless specificity, and the rule of three.

## When to use it
Any headline, subject line, hero H1, or ad hook.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Apply the Four U's**, a strong headline is:
   - Unique (stands out from competitors)
   - Urgent (time pressure or compelling need)
   - Ultra-specific (concrete numbers/details)
   - Useful (clear benefit)
   - Examples: "lightning fast: pages load 312% faster"; "14% less stressed in just 10 days"; Oatly's daring "This tastes like sh*t! Blah!"
2. **Prefer specific over generic:** "pages load 312% faster than what you're using" beats "fastest-loading product"; "212,000 five-star reviews and counting" beats "world's most popular clean makeup."
3. **Use rhyme when natural**, people rate rhyming lines as more likable, memorable, and trustworthy: "Zapier makes you happier"; Headspace's "Be kind to your mind."
4. **Deploy power words** that trigger emotion (fear, encouragement, arousal, anger, greed, safety, curiosity). Patagonia's ad reads as fear/anger top-to-bottom ("screwed," "too late") and as hope bottom-to-top ("choice," "livable," "healthy future").
5. **Group in threes**, the rule of three is the minimum for a recognizable pattern readers latch onto.
6. **Write in second person**, "You will find joy" / "You will catch your breath" beats "Headspace helps with stress."

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Specificity is the highest-leverage edit: swap every vague superlative for a real number.
- Orwell's warning applies: "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity", clarity and honesty are inseparable, don't manufacture urgency you can't back up.

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes a `headline-generator` prompt that outputs 10 variants scored against the Four U's, flags vague words to replace with numbers, and suggests a rhyme option.

### Match your message to the market's sophistication, Schwartz's 5 stages and functional-first for new categories

## The strategy
The same claim lands or flops depending on how much the market has already heard. Eugene Schwartz's 5 stages of market sophistication tell you which message the audience is ready for, from plain "here's what it is" to identity-level "here's who it's for."

## When to use it
Deciding the *angle* for a category, not just the words; entering a crowded market where direct benefit claims are exhausted; launching a genuinely new category.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Diagnose the stage** (issue #156):
   - **Stage 1, Only:** you're first; simply state what you are.
   - **Stage 2, Better:** competitors exist; amplify the benefit ("more/faster/bigger").
   - **Stage 3, How it works:** benefit claims are worn out; lead with the mechanism.
   - **Stage 4, Even Better:** mechanisms are common; amplify *your* mechanism's superiority.
   - **Stage 5, Who it's for:** everything's been said; sell identity, lifestyle, and belonging.
2. **Write the message for the current stage**, don't skip ahead. A Stage-1 audience isn't ready for identity messaging; a Stage-5 audience yawns at basic benefit claims.
3. **For a genuinely new category, lead with functional benefits before emotional appeal** (issue #117). People need to grasp *what it does* before they'll care what it means. Peloton's early headline: "Join studio cycling classes from the comfort of your home."

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Most "our messaging stopped working" problems are a market that advanced a stage while your copy stayed put.
- Stage 5 is where brand and identity work (see the `brand` cards) does the heavy lifting; stages 1-2 are pure clarity.
- Pair with the `reframe-and-salience` card: reframing can effectively reset a saturated Stage-4 market to a fresh Stage-1 problem you own.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: given a category and 3-5 competitor headlines, classify the market's sophistication stage and output a stage-appropriate messaging angle (plus a warning if current copy is written for the wrong stage).

### High-lift micro-copy tweaks, "because" reasons, oddly-specific numbers, present tense, concrete words, conditional claims

## The strategy
A cluster of small, tested word-level swaps that each move conversion or engagement measurably, the kind of edits you apply during a final copy pass without rewriting structure.

## When to use it
Polishing CTAs, prices, reviews, offers, and headlines; a final editing pass before shipping; A/B test ideas when you need cheap, high-signal variants.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Add a "because" reason to any request** (issue #167): the classic photocopier study lifted compliance from 60% → 94% just by adding "because I'm in a rush." Give a reason for every ask, even an obvious one.
2. **Use oddly-specific numbers** in prices, deadlines, and offers (issue #167): run a sale for "37.5 hours," not "24." Specific-amount beggars got 60% more donations than round-number ones. Specificity signals credibility.
3. **Write reviews and copy in present tense** (issue #167): present-tense reviews earned 26.4% more upvotes and +12.3% purchase intent vs. past tense.
4. **Prefer conditional claims over absolutes** (issue #066): a headline using "may" outperformed "will" by 200%. Add "if you do it right," "probably," "when it's time to" to ground promises and reduce skepticism.
5. **Swap vague words for concrete ones** (issue #126): Mutiny doubled homepage conversion by changing the CTA "See it on your site" → "See it on your homepage."
6. **Use proximal-sense (touch/taste) words for impulse buys** (issue #129): "Taste the rainbow" beats "See the rainbow." Distal senses (sight/sound) create psychological distance and delay the purchase.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- These are independent levers, test them one at a time so you can attribute the lift.
- "Because" works even with a weak reason, but a *real* reason compounds the effect; don't fake it.
- Conditional-claim softening (#066) also lowers legal/overpromise risk, a rare win-win.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: paste any copy and auto-suggest micro-edits, insert "because" reasons, replace round numbers with oddly-specific ones, flip past→present tense, swap absolutes for conditionals, and highlight vague words with concrete alternatives.

### Structure copy around tension, not chronology, open with conflict, minimum viable backstory, and open loops

## The strategy
Story sells (see the `story-driven-copy` card for the hero's-journey basics). This card is the craft layer: how to *sequence* a story for maximum pull, start in the conflict, cut the setup, withhold the payoff, and ride emotional beats rather than timeline order.

## When to use it
Video and narrative ads, founder/origin stories, long-form copy, webinar and email scripts, anywhere a reader can bail before the point lands.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Open with conflict, not chronology** (issue #085): map story beats on sticky notes and reorder by *emotional* impact, not time. Hook example: "There's a Rang-tan in my bedroom."
2. **Minimum Viable Backstory** (issues #134, #230, Wes Kao): give just enough context to set the stage, cut the rest. "Start right before you get eaten by the bear." Every detail should either set the scene or drive the narrative.
3. **Anchor in a specific moment** (issue #230): open with vivid action, not context ("The waiter slapped me with a baguette"), use the five senses, and open near the moment of change.
4. **7-part narrative-ad structure** (issue #222): hook in the first 2s (users scroll ~43 ft/day) → minimum viable backstory → withhold the "why" until ~2/3 in → make the viewer the protagonist → use relatable ambiguity + one real stat ("45% of women have had pregnancy scares") → show, don't tell → narrow to a single issue.
5. **Map the emotional arc before writing** (issue #145): outline the emotion at each stage (slow positive build → anxiety → FOMO, or hope → despair → relief). "Buy with emotion, justify with logic."
6. **Insert bucket-brigade transitions** (issue #089) to cut drop-off: "Here's the deal," "But here's the kicker," "Want to know the best part?", placed at transitions and right before key takeaways.
7. Optional template: the Pixar "Once upon a time… every day… until one day… because of that… until finally" 6-part arc (issue #114) for brand/product story scaffolding.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The recurring principle: withhold information to create an open loop; the reader stays to close it.
- Cutting backstory feels risky but almost always improves pull, most drafts front-load too much setup.
- Complements `story-driven-copy` (arc + sensory + real-people voice) with sequencing and tension mechanics.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: paste a raw story/testimonial, then output a reordered version that opens on the conflict, trims to minimum viable backstory, inserts bucket-brigade transitions, and marks where to withhold the "why."

### Preempt objections in the copy and back claims with mined social proof

## The strategy
Decision-making is emotional, and doubt kills conversions. Two moves close the gap: surface and neutralize the reader's top objections before they bail, and prove your claims with specific, mined social proof.

## When to use it
Landing pages, checkout/cart copy, pricing pages, sales emails, anywhere a prospect hesitates.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Find the real objections:** ask customers "what obstacle would have prevented purchase?"; use live chat, surveys, and website forms; read reviews; do social listening; use heatmap tools (Hotjar) to find where people abandon.
2. **Preempt each objection in the copy, early.** Monday.com pattern:
   - "Too complicated" → "Actually easy to use"
   - "Too expensive" → "Free forever. No credit card"
   - "Inflexible" → "Fast no-code customization"
   - "Long onboarding" → "Easy setup"
3. **Mine customer reviews for copy.** Build a spreadsheet with three columns: (1) memorable phrases customers use, (2) what people want, (3) emotional reactions. Reuse the recurring language in your copy.
4. **Layer social proof types:** testimonials/reviews, UGC, media quotes and client logos, star ratings, celebrity/influencer endorsements.
5. **Use reverse testimonials** (Sean D'Souza): open with the objection/skepticism, then resolve it, "I worried Zendesk might not beat competitors. I researched, it's the best."
6. **Make proof specific:** "212,000 five-star reviews and counting" beats "most popular." Name a real person in the CTA, "Give directly to Nyevu" beats "Donate now."

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Specificity is the throughline: named people, exact review counts, and verbatim customer phrases outperform generic claims.
- Reverse testimonials work because they voice the reader's own doubt first, building trust before the resolution lands.

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes an `objection-map` prompt: input a product + review dump, output the top objections, a preempt line for each, and a mined-phrase social-proof block.

### Make your value prop unstealable, the Swap Test, one defensible claim, and Dunford's positioning process

## The strategy
Most value props are benefits any competitor could also claim. Real positioning is what survives when a rival swaps their logo in. This card is a diagnostic-plus-build loop: test your claims for defensibility, keep only the ownable ones, and rebuild messaging around them.

## When to use it
Homepage rewrites, positioning resets, "our messaging isn't landing," pitch decks, or before spending on ads that repeat undifferentiated benefits.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Run the Swap Test:** swap a competitor's name into your value prop. If it still reads true, it's a benefit, not a differentiator. Same idea as the "job test", if 3 different roles could all think a claim is for them, it's too generic.
2. **Catalog every alternative**, including spreadsheets, incumbents, and doing-nothing/inaction, and write down how each one fails the customer. Respond to each failure. Keep only **2-3 ownable claims** you can defend.
3. **Diagnose messaging into 4 buckets before rewriting** (issue #302): **Audience** (name a specific role, "RevOps leaders," not "businesses"), **Problem** (anchor to the downstream consequence, "deadlines slip, I look bad," not "approvals get lost"), **Value Prop** (the swap-your-logo test above; build a 3-column table: alternatives → your unique edge → who cares most), **Sequence** (what order beliefs must form).
4. **Pick ONE defensible differentiation claim** (issue #275): faster/cheaper, unique access, a different approach, a deliberate tradeoff ("we only serve Series A"), or niche dominance. Don't spread across five.
5. **Build a "narrative kernel"** (issue #306) by mining 10-20 customer-language sources (sales calls, support tickets, reviews, community posts). Highlight repeating complaints/outcomes/criticisms in the customer's own words, then distill to a one-line template: *"We exist so [X] can finally [Y] without [Z]."*
6. For a longer process, run April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* 12 steps (issue #144): identify your most eager customers → their real alternatives → your verifiably superior features → match features to segments → target the most underserved segment → write a team reference doc so everyone positions identically.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Proof of impact (issue #339): Stacker doubled homepage conversions and lifted ad CTR +22%; Perfect Keto +30% from messaging alone; Unspun's "jeans in one size, yours."
- Seedlip repositioned from ~$2 tonic-water pricing to gin-bottle price points purely by changing the reference category (Dunford, #144).
- Tool to try: Rocksalt, which surfaces real LinkedIn buyer conversations for customer language.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: paste a value prop + a competitor name and auto-run the Swap Test + job test, flag every claim that survives with a rival's logo, and output the 3-column differentiation table with a suggested narrative-kernel one-liner.

### Reframe the category's problem instead of restating it, the Reframe Ladder, Rule of One, and dangling tension

## The strategy
When copy "isn't landing," the usual instinct is to polish the words. Often it's a salience problem, not a copy problem: you're restating the category's default narrative, so nothing stands out. The fix is to reframe the underlying problem to one only you solve, and to make each asset carry exactly one idea.

## When to use it
Crowded categories where everyone says the same thing; homepages that read like a pitch deck; ads that list 5-7 features; copy that describes the product accurately but converts poorly.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Run the 4-step Reframe Ladder** (issue #300): (1) name the category default (what everyone in the space says); (2) find the deeper unmet tension underneath it; (3) expose the misalignment between the default framing and what the customer actually needs; (4) define a problem only *you* solve, then message against that problem.
2. **Apply the Rule of One** (issue #301): for every asset ask "what is the ONE belief this creates?" If you can't say it in a single line, the asset is doing too much. This is the antidote to homepage-as-pitch-deck, feature-stuffed ads, and demo-before-awareness asks.
3. **Dangle the outcome; don't explain the mechanism** (issue #298): create tension by promising the result and withholding the "how." VideoGen's "Your grandma can be an influencer" (no explanation) beat feature-led copy. Deliberately stop copy mid-explanation to drive the click.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- The Reframe Ladder is a strategic move, not a word tweak, it changes *which problem you sell against*, upstream of any headline.
- Pair with the Swap Test card: reframing gives you a problem only you own; the Swap Test keeps your value-prop claims defensible.
- Tension only works if the withheld payoff is worth the reach, a dangled outcome the reader doesn't want falls flat.

## → Skill conversion note
Skill: given a category and a product, generate the "category default" narrative, propose 2-3 deeper tensions, and output a reframed one-problem statement plus a Rule-of-One check on the current homepage copy.

### Sell with story, use the hero's-journey arc, sensory detail, and authentic real-people voice

## The strategy
People remember stories better than facts, and stories create the emotional connection that drives action. Pair a narrative arc with authentic, real-people voice (the modern shift from aspirational fantasy to affirmational reality).

## When to use it
About pages, founder/origin stories, launch copy, brand campaigns, long-form emails, anywhere connection matters more than a spec sheet.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Build on the hero's-journey arc:** exposition (situation) → rising action (complications) → climax (turning point) → falling action (resolution) → denouement (outcome).
2. **Include the story ingredients:** emotional resonance, a human character to root for, literary devices (symmetry, contrast, rhyme, pacing), sensory detail, suspense, and a hero vs. a common-enemy villain.
3. **Use pacing and suspense**, delay the payoff/headline to build anticipation. Cheetos ran 33 words before revealing the product; email subject lines should read like cliffhangers.
4. **Shift from aspirational to affirmational**, show real, ordinary people, not idealized models. This shift traces from Dove's "Campaign for Real Beauty" to Schick's "Be You". Roughly 85% of men prefer real people over idealized models in ads.
5. **Humanize the brand:** share UGC, get personal (founder stories, candid moments), keep it unscripted/authentic, and speak the audience's conversational language.
6. **Use humor deliberately**, only when it never sacrifices clarity, credibility is proven separately, it fits the brand voice and the audience's culture/geography, and virality is a goal. Avoid forced, insensitive, or clarity-killing jokes. El Arroyo built a 570k+ Instagram following on marquee humor + authenticity.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Who Gives A Crap origin story: co-founder Simon sat on a toilet for 50 hours in a drafty warehouse ("one cold bottom") to fund the launch, the story helped drive $50k in pre-orders.
- Orwell's clarity principle underpins all of it: insincerity breeds bloated language; authentic stories stay clear. Summary tag: "Clear eyes, full hearts, can't lose."

## → Skill conversion note
Becomes a `story-copy` prompt: input founder/product facts, output an origin narrative structured on the hero's-journey arc with a sensory hook and a delayed-payoff opening.

### Story Systems, a 7-part brand narrative and messaging framework

## The strategy
Story Systems is a framework for building one coherent brand narrative that aligns the whole company and cascades consistent messaging across every funnel stage. It's a 7-part system delivered over ~3 weeks in 4 phases.

## When to use it
When messaging is inconsistent across ads/site/email/sales, or a brand needs a single narrative foundation before scaling content.

## How to execute (steps), the 7 components
1. Core Story, the single thread the whole company aligns on: why you exist, who you help, why it matters.
2. Brand Archetype & Voice Guide, personality + consistency across all communications.
3. Persona Profiles, audience understanding expressed in the words they actually use.
4. Core Problems Framework, the deepest pains beneath the obvious ones.
5. Value Propositions, solutions framed as a future they want, in claims you can own.
6. Hook Library, sharp openers that cut through noise and earn attention.
7. Journey Messaging Map, stage-specific messaging for consistency across the funnel.

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Process = 4 phases over 3 weeks: (1) Find your story (what's true, different, and yours alone); (2) Make it impossible to ignore (hooks); (3) Build emotional resonance (connect value to buyer pain); (4) Deploy across the journey (ads, landing pages, emails, sales, onboarding).

### Write copy from mined customer language, S.C.A.R.F. research, a 10-angle messaging matrix, and competitor mining

## The strategy
The best copy is transcribed, not invented, it uses the customer's own words for their problems and desires. This card is the research system that feeds every other copy card: where to mine language and how to turn it into ranked messaging angles.

## When to use it
Before any homepage, ad, or email rewrite; onboarding a new product or client; when copy feels like it was written by the founder instead of the buyer.

## How to execute (steps)
1. **Run S.C.A.R.F. voice-of-customer research** (issue #142): **Social** (10 problem words pulled from posts), **Competitors** (5 copy samples + their Google Ads via SEMrush), **Affiliates** (the language affiliates use to pitch you), **Reviews** (1-3★ for objections, 4-5★ for benefits), **Forums** (Reddit pain points).
2. **Build a 10-angle messaging matrix from reviews** (issue #090): list 25 reasons to buy, tally benefit mentions across reviews by *frequency*, then match the top 10 most-mentioned benefits to your "why buy" list. Frequency ranks the angles for you.
3. **Mine competitor influencer posts for copy** (issue #082): Google `site:instagram.com #[competitor] #ad` or `@[brand] #ad`, extract 10-15 resonant phrases, and A/B test adapted versions.
4. **Collect "relatable truths"** (issue #201) into buckets, context (LA traffic), holidays/events, industry pain, cultural moments (AI/crypto/remote work/memes), human psychology, so copy makes the audience nod along. Keep a running list for future campaigns.
5. **Voice an unvoiced frustration** (issue #127): name a widely-felt-but-unspoken gripe so readers think "finally, someone said it", this triggers organic sharing (Chris Donnelly's virtue-signaling post).

## Notes / caveats / examples
- Frequency of mention (step 2) is the cheapest prioritization signal you have, the market has already voted in the reviews.
- Feeds the `objection-handling-and-social-proof`, `positioning-swap-test`, and `benefit-of-the-benefit` cards, they all consume mined customer language.
- Keep quotes close to verbatim; paraphrasing sands off the specificity that makes VoC copy work.

## → Skill conversion note
Strong skill: point it at a product's review pages + a competitor handle, then auto-run S.C.A.R.F., tally benefit frequency, and output a ranked 10-angle messaging matrix with verbatim customer phrases.
